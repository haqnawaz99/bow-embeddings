## What this notebook covers

N-grams extend BoW by treating sequences of N consecutive words as single features. "not good" becomes one token instead of two unrelated ones. You will explore bigram features, build a simple bigram language model, and see exactly where n-grams still fall short.

## What the previous approach got wrong

Both BoW and TF-IDF are order-blind. "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce identical representations. Negation ("not good"), phrases ("New York"), and idioms are all invisible.

## Key concept

**N-gram**: a contiguous sequence of N words treated as a single feature.

- Unigram: "mercy"
- Bigram: "have mercy"
- Trigram: "have mercy on"

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
```

## Topics covered

- Unigram vs bigram feature spaces
- `ngram_range` in `CountVectorizer` and `TfidfVectorizer`
- The stopword trap: removing stopwords breaks meaningful bigrams like "not good"
- Bigram language model: P(word | previous word)
- Bigram feature inspection: most informative pairs
- Limitations table: what n-grams still cannot do

## Limitation this notebook reveals

N-grams capture local word sequences but still have no semantic understanding. "have mercy" and "show compassion" are completely unrelated features in an n-gram model. The vocabulary explodes (bigrams + trigrams = millions of features) but meaning is still missing.

Word embeddings in NB04 replace the entire sparse feature space with dense vectors that encode meaning.
